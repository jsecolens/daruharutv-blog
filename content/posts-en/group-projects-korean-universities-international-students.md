Your first group assignment begins. A chat room appears, classmates start discussing ideas, and you are still working out when to introduce yourself.

If you are studying in another language, group work can feel demanding before the actual project has even started. The challenge is not simply vocabulary. You also need to understand what the group has decided, what you are responsible for, and when others expect your work.

There is no single way that "Korean students" do group projects. Classes, instructors, and individual teams differ. Clear agreements are more useful than assumptions about national culture.

---

## Start with a short introduction and a practical question

You do not need a perfect introduction. Share your name, what you can contribute, and any communication need that affects collaboration.

For example:

> Hi, I'm [name]. I can help with research and organizing the slides. I would appreciate it if we could also record important decisions and deadlines in the chat.

Mentioning a language need is not the same as asking others to do your work. A written recap can help everyone, including people who miss a meeting.

If you prefer to draft in English, ask how the group can incorporate your contribution into the required submission language. Agree on that process before the deadline gets close.

---

## Read the assignment together before dividing the work

Confirm what the instructor is actually assessing: a report, a presentation, a shared product, individual contributions, or some combination. Check the submission format, time limit, citation requirements, and any group evaluation process stated in the instructions.

Then divide the work into specific deliverables. "Help with research" is difficult to track. "Find three relevant sources and write a 200-word summary by Wednesday evening" is much clearer.

Use a shared table such as this:

| Task | Person responsible | Internal deadline | Completion means |
|---|---|---|---|
| Research | Assigned member | Agreed date and time | Sources linked and key findings summarized |
| Draft | Assigned member | Agreed date and time | Required sections written in the shared document |
| Slides | Assigned member | Agreed date and time | Slides organized and evidence cited |
| Final check and submission | Named checker and submitter | Before the official cutoff | File checked, uploaded, and receipt shared |

Roles can overlap or rotate. The important point is that everyone knows what the agreement means. Set an internal deadline that leaves time to combine and review the work.

---

## Make decisions and progress visible

After a discussion, post a short recap: what was decided, who will do what, and the next deadline. If something is unclear, ask while there is still time to change it.

Useful phrases include:

> I would like to check that I understood correctly. Should I complete [task] by [date and time]?

> I think this part is still undecided. Could we decide on it today?

Agree on a communication channel that everyone can access. Also discuss reasonable response expectations. Being in a group chat does not automatically mean that everyone is available at all hours.

---

## Raise difficulties early and describe the specific problem

If you cannot finish your part, tell the group what is complete, what is delayed, and what revised plan you propose. Silence makes coordination harder.

If the workload seems uneven, refer to the task list rather than blaming someone's personality or background:

> My current tasks are [list]. I think we need to adjust the roles to finish by the deadline. Could we redistribute some of the work?

If a serious problem continues, use the instructor's stated process and explain the situation with relevant records. Avoid circulating unrelated private messages. Assessment disputes should follow the course's procedure.

---

## Agree on AI use before combining everyone's work

Check the instructor's rules before using AI for translation, drafting, coding, or other parts of the assignment. An individual member's preference does not determine what is permitted for the group submission.

Check the rules for your own class, including any requirements to verify AI-generated content or disclose AI assistance. Do not assume that the same policy applies to every course.

Where AI is allowed, agree on how its use will be recorded. Check claims and references yourself, and do not upload classmates' personal information or restricted materials without an appropriate basis.

Before submitting, rehearse the presentation, check the final file, and confirm who will upload it. Share the submission confirmation so the entire group knows the task is complete.

Successful group work does not require everyone to communicate in exactly the same way. It requires a shared understanding of the task—and enough clarity for each person to contribute.

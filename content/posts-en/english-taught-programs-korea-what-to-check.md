You find a degree program in Korea advertised as English-taught. That sounds like an answer to an important question: "Can I study there if my Korean is still limited?"

It may be a very good option. But choosing a program involves more than checking the language of lectures. You also need to understand how the degree works and what support is available when you step outside the classroom.

The aim is to find a program that matches both your academic goals and your day-to-day needs.

---

## First, clarify what "English-taught" covers

Start by checking the scope of the language commitment in the program's official description. The phrase "English-taught" needs to be read alongside the curriculum and the requirements for the specific degree you want to pursue.

Ask what the university's description means in practice. Does it refer to the whole degree, a particular track, or selected courses? Check the curriculum and recent course offerings rather than relying only on a promotional heading.

If you are an exchange student, confirm whether those courses are open to your student category. An English-taught course may exist without being available to every visiting student.

---

## Check whether you can complete the degree as planned

A useful question is not only "Are there English courses?" but "Can I complete the required courses in English within my intended study period?"

Ask about required major courses, general education, prerequisites, and how often essential courses are offered. Course availability can affect your plan even when the teaching language is clear.

Check the graduation rules for your program and entry year. If there is a thesis, capstone, internship, examination, or language requirement, ask what applies to you and where it is documented. Do not assume that another student's experience or an older brochure describes your own requirements. Look for the requirements specific to your major, not just a general university overview.

---

## Ask about communication beyond lectures

Teaching, assessment, and administration are related but separate areas. Before enrolling, find out how the university handles the following:

| Area | A practical question |
|---|---|
| Lectures and materials | What language is used for teaching, readings, and course announcements? |
| Assessment | What language is required for assignments, presentations, and examinations? |
| Group projects | How are shared materials and assessed work handled in multilingual groups? |
| Academic advice | Can I discuss course selection and graduation planning in English? |
| Administration | Are key notices and procedures available in English, and who can help if they are not? |
| Student support | What language support is available for housing, counseling, and other services? |

These are questions to investigate, not claims that English support is missing. The answer may differ between programs within the same university.

---

## Think about the life you want outside class

Consider your own plans: joining a club, looking for an internship, volunteering, or handling daily errands independently. Ask about the language used in activities that matter to you.

Learning practical Korean may make some interactions easier, but that is different from saying that every English-taught program requires Korean proficiency. Formal admission and graduation requirements must come from the program's official documents.

You can set a manageable personal goal instead: learn how to ask for clarification, read a room notice, or describe a basic problem. Language learning does not have to be all-or-nothing.

---

## Send questions that produce specific answers

Instead of asking "Is your university suitable for foreigners?", try this message:

> Hello, I am considering [program] for [intake]. Could you confirm whether all courses required for this degree can be completed in English? I would also appreciate links to the current curriculum, graduation requirements, and information about academic advising and administrative support in English. Are there any requirements or course access restrictions specific to my student category?

General study-abroad resources can help you build a shortlist, but check the institution's own program information before deciding. Keep links to the current curriculum and admissions guide so you can compare programs using the same questions.

An English-taught program can open a meaningful path to studying in Korea. Knowing what its language commitment covers—and how the rest of university life works—helps you choose with clearer expectations.

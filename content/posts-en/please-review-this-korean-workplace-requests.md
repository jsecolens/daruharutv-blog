A colleague sends you a file and says, "Please review this." You read it, think it looks fine, and move on. Later, the colleague asks, "Have you finished the changes?"

You understood the words, but you understood different tasks.

This can happen in any workplace. When you are also working in a second language, it helps to have a few short questions ready. You do not need a complicated reply—just enough to find out what the other person wants.

---

## 1. "Please review this" — ask what needs checking

The person may want you to find mistakes, check numbers, or change the wording. Instead of guessing, ask:

> "Should I just check it, or would you like me to make changes?"

If it is a long document, ask which part matters most:

> "Would you like me to check the whole document or just the prices?"

For example, a colleague sending a quotation may only need you to confirm the prices. There is no reason to spend an hour rewriting the introduction if that is not what they need.

It also helps to ask how to return your feedback. Should you change the file itself, leave comments, or send a short message? If you find only one mistake, a message may be enough. If several pages need changing, an edited copy may be easier for the other person to use. Agreeing on this first saves both of you another round of work.

---

## 2. "For your reference" — usually read it, then check if action is needed

Sometimes a message is simply information you should know. You may not need to write a report or send anything back.

But if the information changes something you are already working on, a short question helps:

> "Thanks. Should I update my document with this information?"

Imagine receiving a new meeting time. If you are attending, update your calendar. If you are organizing the meeting, check whether you also need to tell everyone else.

The same message can lead to different actions depending on your role.

---

## 3. "Could you do this?" — check the deadline before promising

A polite question may be asking whether you have time, or it may be asking you to start the work. You can reply naturally:

> "Yes, I can help. When do you need it?"

If you already have another task, say so:

> "I am finishing the report now. Should I do this first, or finish the report before starting?"

This gives your manager a clear choice. It is better than quietly trying to finish two urgent jobs at once.

If you do not know how long the new task will take, say, "Let me check first. I will get back to you this afternoon."

If you realise later that you will be late, send an update before the agreed time. Explain what is ready and what still needs work: "The first two pages are finished, but I am waiting for the figures for page three. Would you like the completed pages now?" This gives the other person something useful to work with instead of leaving them wondering where the file is.

---

## 4. "As soon as possible" — ask for a time

"As soon as possible" can mean very different things to different people. Ask a practical question:

> "Would tomorrow morning be okay, or do you need it today?"

After agreeing, send one short sentence confirming what you will do:

> "Okay, I will check the prices and send the corrected file by 3 p.m. today."

Now both people know what to expect.

When you finish, say what you completed: "I checked the prices and corrected two items. The updated file is attached." This is clearer than sending a file with no explanation.

---

## 5. After a quick conversation, send a quick recap

A request made in a hallway or during a phone call is easy to forget. When you return to your desk, write one sentence in your usual work chat: "As discussed, I will update the meeting slides and send them tomorrow morning." If you heard something incorrectly, the other person can correct it early. Keep it friendly and brief; you are simply making the plan easier to remember.

You do not have to read someone's mind to work well in Korea. Asking "What should I change?" and "When do you need it?" is often enough to prevent a lot of unnecessary work.
